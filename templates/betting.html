How to read this page

The idea. The model gives each team a chance to win. A bookmaker's price implies a chance too. When the model's chance times the price comes to more than 1, the bet looks profitable if the model is right: that's the "edge". Example: the model gives a team 75.6% at a price of −135 (decimal 1.741): 0.756 × 1.741 − 1 = +31.6% expected per $1.

Columns. Price: American odds (−135 = risk $135 to win $100; +150 = risk $100 to win $150). Model and Market: each one's chance for the pick (the market's with the bookmaker's margin removed). Edge: the expected return if the model is right; bets need {{ min_edge }} or more. Rule's stake: the Kelly formula for your bankroll, scaled by the fraction you pick and capped at {{ cap }}. Recommended: what the site actually stands behind, $0 unless the sport's past test made money.

Read big edges with suspicion. A 20-point disagreement with the market usually means the model is missing something (a late scratch, a trade, rest), not that the market is wrong. In past seasons, the bigger the edge, the worse it did.

Is it working? Two numbers to watch in the live record: units (profit with $1 on every bet: noisy, needs hundreds of bets) and closing-line value (did the price move toward the pick before the game: a faster, steadier sign of a real edge). The past-season table's 95% range says how far luck alone could move a result; if it's all below 0, the loss isn't bad luck.

In one sentence: the site flags bets where its chances disagree with the odds, sizes them with the Kelly formula, and logs them publicly so they can't be cherry-picked; tested honestly, they lose, because the betting market is more accurate than the model.

What we tried to make it better

Each change was tried on the earlier seasons first, then checked once on the held-out seasons it never saw. Only betting at the best price helped, and only in the Premier League (the one sport with more than one bookmaker's price); it's what the rule uses now. Nothing turned a loss into a profit.

Blend mixes the model's chance with the market's before looking for an edge, weighted by what predicted the earlier seasons best. It backfired: with the chances pulled toward the market, the only edges left were long shots, where the bookmaker's margin is biggest, and those lost. Tuned rules looked good on the seasons they were tuned on (the grey rows) and fell apart on new ones, which is what overfitting looks like. The blend's held-out log loss is no better than the market's alone: the models don't know anything the prices don't already include. Better bets need better information, not a cleverer rule.

Spreads and totals

The models predict each side's points (or goals), so they give a chance for every point spread and over/under too, not just for who wins. The same rule (an edge of 3% or more, a quarter of Kelly) was tried on those markets on the held-out seasons. It doesn't work: the model's chances are less accurate than the market's in every one, so the rule bets moneylines only.

How the stakes are worked out

Expected return, Kelly and why only a fraction

Expected return. A bet at decimal price d (American +150 is 2.50) on a result with chance p returns p × d − 1 per $1 on average. The rule suggests a bet when that's {{ min_edge }} or more by the model's chances, at most one bet per game.

Kelly. If the chances were exactly right, staking (p × d − 1) / (d − 1) of your bankroll grows it fastest over the long run. But the chances are estimates, and full Kelly on overestimated edges loses money fast (see the past seasons above), so the rule uses a fraction of it ({{ (fraction * 100)|round|int }}% here) and never more than {{ cap }} of the bankroll on one bet.

Flat stakes. The live record counts $1 on every bet ("units"), the simplest fair test.
